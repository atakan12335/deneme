# Time-to-Proficiency and Learning Methods for an Adult Turkish Speaker Learning English to Live and Work in the USA

Research notes, compiled 22 Oct 2026. Sources accessed Oct 2026 unless a publication date is given. Two primary sources could not be fetched directly because of Cloudflare blocks: the Cambridge English support page and the Council of Europe (coe.int) CEFR pages. Cambridge figures are cross-checked through search snippets and secondary sources. The official CEFR global-scale descriptors are quoted from EF SET pages that reproduce them.

**Correction to the brief:** the US Foreign Service Institute (FSI) does NOT list Turkish as Category IV. As of Oct 2026, state.gov lists Turkish in **Category III ("hard languages", ~44 weeks / 1,012 class hours)**. Category IV ("super-hard": 88 weeks / 2,200 class hours) covers only Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese and Korean. See Q2.

## Q1. CEFR levels A1–C2: what a person can do, and what level is needed for US life, work, university and near-native use

### Takeaway
Daily survival in the US is possible at A2–B1. B2 is the realistic minimum for working in English (with gaps in nuance). C1 gives full professional/academic autonomy and matches most US university entry scores (TOEFL iBT ~95+ / IELTS 6.5+). C2 is "essentially native" and few learners need it.

### Cited Findings
**Official CEFR global-scale descriptors (as reproduced by EF SET), with EF's practical interpretation:**
- **A1 (Beginner):** "Can understand and use familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases… Can introduce herself and others… Can interact… in a simple way provided the other person talks slowly and clearly and is prepared to help." EF: enough for "very simple interactions, for example as a tourist… would not be sufficient for other academic or professional purposes." — [EF SET A1](https://www.efset.org/cefr/a1/)
- **A2 (Elementary):** "Can understand sentences and frequently used expressions related to areas of most immediate relevance (e.g. very basic personal and family information, shopping, local geography, employment). Can communicate in simple and routine tasks…" EF: "sufficient for tourism… and socializing… although to develop deeper friendships an A2 level is not adequate… working in English is limited to very familiar topics… not sufficient for academic study or for consuming most English-language media." — [EF SET A2](https://www.efset.org/cefr/a2/)
- **B1 (Intermediate):** "Can understand the main points of clear standard input on familiar matters regularly encountered in work, school, leisure… Can deal with most situations likely to arise whilst travelling… Can describe experiences and events, dreams, hopes and ambitions…" EF: at work, B1 learners "are able to read simple reports on familiar topics and write simple e-mails… However, a B1 level is not adequate to function fully in the workplace in English." Also "not able to work or study exclusively in English." — [EF SET B1](https://www.efset.org/cefr/b1/)
- **B2 (Upper intermediate):** "Can understand the main ideas of complex text… including technical discussions in his/her field… Can interact with a degree of fluency and spontaneity that makes regular interaction with native speakers quite possible without strain for either party." EF: "would allow you to function in the workplace in English… many non-native English speakers in international workplaces have this level… will, however, lack nuance particularly outside his own field… may also miss some of the subtleties and implied meanings." — [EF SET B2](https://www.efset.org/cefr/b2/)
- **C1 (Advanced):** "Can understand a wide range of demanding, longer texts, and recognize implicit meaning. Can express ideas fluently and spontaneously without much obvious searching for expressions. Can use language flexibly and effectively for social, academic and professional purposes." EF: "allows for a full range of functionality at work or in an academic setting… would allow for full autonomy in a native English-speaking country." — [EF SET C1](https://www.efset.org/cefr/c1/)
- **C2 (Proficient):** "Can understand with ease virtually everything heard or read… differentiating finer shades of meaning even in the most complex situations." EF: "essentially a native level… A well-educated native English speaker is technically at a C2 level. Relatively few English learners reach this level because their professional or academic goals do not require it." — [EF SET C2](https://www.efset.org/cefr/c2/)
- CEFR levels are broad. "A student who has just reached the B1 level is quite a long way behind a student who has almost… mastered all of the B2 skills, but both… would be defined as… B1." — [EF SET: The CEFR and EF SET](https://www.efset.org/cefr/)

**US university entry requirements:**
- Typical US requirements are about IELTS 6.5–7.0 or TOEFL iBT 80–100. Top-tier universities (MIT, Stanford, Harvard) "often require IELTS 7.0+ or TOEFL 100+." This is a third-party guide. — [ielts.international](https://www.ielts.international/en/ielts-vs-toefl/usa)

**Turkey baseline (relevant to the end user):**

By job: IT 513, R&D 519, Accounting & Finance 445. — [EF EPI Türkiye](https://www.ef.com/wwen/epi/regions/europe/turkey/); [EF EPI 2025 Türkiye fact sheet (PDF)](https://www.ef.com/assetscdn/WIBIwq6RdJvcD9bc8RMd/cefcom-epi-site/fact-sheets/2025/ef-epi-fact-sheet-turkey-english.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Practical US mapping:**
  - Getting around (shopping, doctor with help, small talk): A2–B1.
  - Typical job (service, trades, many technical jobs): B1+ to B2.
  - White-collar/professional job (meetings, email, negotiating, client-facing): solid B2, ideally C1.
  - University: B2+ to C1. TOEFL 80 sits mid-B2 and TOEFL 100 / IELTS 7.0 sits in C1, per the EF concordance.
  - "Near-native": C2, plus accent work, which CEFR does not measure directly.
- Turkish adults typically have reading/listening ahead of speaking/writing (EF EPI 2025 gap of ~60 points). A Turkish learner who tests "B1" on a receptive-only test may be closer to A2 in speaking. Plans should overweight output.

### Gaps
- Could not access the CEFR Companion Volume (2020) at coe.int (blocked). It includes new descriptors such as mediation, online interaction and phonological control. The descriptors above are the 2001 global scale as reproduced by EF.
- No authoritative source found stating "CEFR level X is legally or officially required" for US employment generally. US immigration and employment have no CEFR requirement. Needs are job-specific.
- ACTFL ↔ CEFR official concordance not retrieved.

## Q2. Guided learning hours per CEFR level (Cambridge, French-language sources, ACTFL) and the FSI categories, reinterpreted for a Turkish speaker

### Takeaway
Cambridge's widely cited guideline is cumulative guided (taught) hours from zero: A2 ≈ 180–200, B1 ≈ 350–400, B2 ≈ 500–600, C1 ≈ 700–800, C2 ≈ 1,000–1,200. That is roughly 200 guided hours per level. Total effort including self-study is roughly 1.75–2× higher. FSI places Turkish in Category III (1,012 class hours plus self-study, about 1,760 total hours, to reach ILR 3 for an English speaker). By the symmetry of linguistic distance, that is a reasonable upper-bound anchor for a Turkish speaker learning English to professional level.

### Cited Findings
**Cambridge English:**
- Cumulative guided learning hours: A2 ~180–200; B1 ~350–400; B2 ~500–600; C1 ~700–800; C2 ~1,000–1,200. Hours "are cumulative (they do not refer to progressing from one level to the next)". They are a "guideline only. You may require more or less time and support depending on your own needs." — [Cambridge English Support: Guided learning hours](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours) (page blocked to direct fetch; figures confirmed via search snippet and secondary reproductions such as [EC English B2 guide](https://ecenglish.com/en/blog/english/how-long-to-learn-english-to-b2-level/) and [HKU SPACE](https://hkuspace.hku.hk/sharing/common-european-framework-of-reference-for-languages))
- Cambridge also states it takes "approximately 200 guided learning hours… to progress from one level of the CEFR to the next." — [Cambridge English Support (search snippet)](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506)
- A1 ≈ 90–100 hours appears in some secondary sources. Another source calls ~70–100 h for A1 a widely used estimate, not an official Cambridge figure. — [Memrise CEFR levels explained](https://explore.memrise.com/cefr-levels-explained) (secondary; treat as approximate)
- "Guided learning hours" means taught/supervised time. French-language sources note personal revision and practice "often roughly double the total." — [Digischool (FR)](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/combien-de-temps-pour-apprendre-le-francais/)

**French / Alliance Française-type estimates (for French as a foreign language; no official Alliance Française table found):**
- Cumulative estimates: A1 ≈ 60–100 h, A2 ≈ 160–200 h, B1 ≈ 360–400 h, B2 ≈ 560–650 h, C1 ≈ 800–1,000 h. — [Study-abroad.org (FR)](https://www.study-abroad.org/fr/blog/apprendre-francais-etudiants-guide/); [Digischool (FR)](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/combien-de-temps-pour-apprendre-le-francais/)
- The CCFS-Sorbonne guide gives A1 ≈ 90–100 up to C2 ≈ 1,000–1,200, with B2 500–600 and C1 700–800, essentially the Cambridge scale. — [CCFS Sorbonne](https://www.ccfs-sorbonne.fr/en/?p=100426)

**FSI (US State Department, National Foreign Affairs Training Center):**
- The timelines indicate "the time usually expected for a student to reach an integrated score of 3 (Speaking + Listening) on the Interagency Language Roundtable (ILR) scale, though the actual time can vary based on several factors, including the language learner's natural ability, prior language-learning experience, and time spent in the classroom. **A typical week is 23 hours per week in class and 17 hours of self-study.**" — [state.gov: Foreign Language Training](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/)
- **Category I:** 24–30 weeks (552–690 class hours), "languages close to English" (Spanish/French 30 wks; Dutch, Italian, Portuguese, Swedish etc. 24 wks). **Category II:** ~36 weeks (828 class hours): German, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili, Haitian Creole. **Category III:** ~44 weeks (1,012 class hours), "Hard languages – Languages with significant linguistic and/or cultural differences from English". The list includes **Turkish**, Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Russian, Greek, Hindi, Vietnamese and others. **Category IV:** 88 weeks (2,200 class hours), "Super-hard languages": Arabic, Cantonese, Mandarin, Japanese, Korean. — [state.gov](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/)

**Practitioner estimate that adjusts for native language (Refold, practitioner opinion):**
- Refold groups languages by distance from the learner's native language and says "if you speak a different language, your version of this chart will look very different." Hours to "functional fluency" ("comfortably live your life in the target language… You will still sound like a foreigner and make plenty of mistakes"): cousin 800 h, similar 1,350 h, other 1,700 h, distant 2,700 h. For immigration or university, "maybe multiply the number by 1.5"; for small talk, "around 50%". — [Refold: How Long Does It Take to Learn a Language](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language)

### Inferences
- **How to read FSI for Turkish speakers learning English.** FSI measures English speakers learning other languages, so it does not directly give "Turkish → English" data. Linguistic distance is largely symmetric: Turkish is agglutinative and SOV with vowel harmony, while English is largely analytic, SVO and has articles (this is general linguistic knowledge, not sourced in this session). On that basis, English is plausibly a "Category III-like" language for a Turkish speaker: about 1,012 class hours, or about 1,760 total hours at FSI's 23+17 h/week ratio, to reach ILR 3 (roughly professional working proficiency).
- Several factors make the reverse direction somewhat easier than FSI suggests:
  - Turkish learners usually have years of school English.
  - Both languages use the Latin alphabet.
  - Turks get heavy exposure to English media and tech vocabulary.
  - English learning materials are abundant.
- Others make it harder:
  - English spelling and pronunciation is irregular.
  - English uses articles and phrasal verbs.
  - English vowel contrasts and the th/w sounds are difficult.
  - FSI learners are selected, aptitude-tested, full-time, and in small classes.
- Net estimate: about 1,000–1,800 total hours from true zero to a C1-ish professional level. That range triangulates with Cambridge × 2 (C1 ≈ 1,400–1,600 h), FSI Cat III total (~1,760 h), and Refold "other language" (1,700 h).
- ILR 3 is commonly treated as roughly comparable to CEFR C1 (B2+/C1). There is no official one-to-one concordance (see Gaps).
- Use Cambridge GLH as a lower bound (efficient, teacher-guided hours). Use ×1.75 (FSI's 40/23 ratio) to ×2 (French-source rule) for realistic total hours including homework, review and input.

### Gaps
- No official ILR↔CEFR concordance retrieved in this session.
- ACTFL hour estimates per proficiency level not found or verified.
- No FSI-style data exists for Turkish speakers learning English (reverse direction). The Turkish-specific adjustment above is reasoning, not measured data.
- The Cambridge support page could not be fetched directly (Cloudflare). Its date of last update is unknown.

## Q3. Realistic timelines at 1, 2 and 4+ hours per day

### Takeaway
From zero, the efficient "guided-hours" floor for B2 is ~8–10 months at 2 h/day, but realistic total effort (×2) is ~17–20 months at 2 h/day. C1 takes ~2 years at 2 h/day and ~4 years at 1 h/day. Most Turkish adults start around A2 from school, which cuts these times by roughly 200–400 hours. Above ~4–5 h/day, returns diminish (practitioner claim).

### Cited Findings
- Cambridge cumulative GLH (see Q2): A2 180–200, B1 350–400, B2 500–600, C1 700–800, C2 1,000–1,200. — [Cambridge English Support](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours)
- Self-study roughly doubles total hours relative to taught hours. — [Digischool (FR)](https://www.digischool.fr/articles/fle/combien-de-temps-pour-apprendre-le-francais/)
- FSI: 23 h class + 17 h self-study per week, so 40 h/week. Category III takes 44 weeks (≈1,760 total hours) to reach ILR 3. — [state.gov](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/)
- Refold's "fun threshold" (where native content becomes enjoyable) is about 20% of the functional-fluency hours: cousin 160 h, similar 270 h, other 340 h, distant 540 h. At 2 h/day that is about 3 / 4.5 / 6 / 9 months. "Learners start to lose effectiveness beyond 5 hours per day" ("We don't know if there's really a limit"). — [Refold](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language) (practitioner opinion)
- Lampariello: a "B1 in three months" plan is "only realistic if your target language is similar to your native language"; for English speakers learning Japanese, "9-12 months." — [Luca Lampariello](https://www.lucalampariello.com/how-to-learn-a-language/) (practitioner opinion)

### Inferences
**Timeline table (calculated in these notes from the cited figures; assumes 7 days/week, 30-day months, starting from true zero).** The first number in each cell uses Cambridge guided hours (lower bound: efficient, well-structured study). The second number, after the arrow, uses ×2 realistic total effort.

| A2 | 180–200 → 360–400 | 6–7 mo → 12–13 mo | 3–3.3 mo → 6–6.7 mo | 1.5–1.7 mo → 3–3.3 mo |
| B1 | 350–400 → 700–800 | 11.7–13.3 mo → 23–27 mo | 5.8–6.7 mo → 11.7–13.3 mo | 2.9–3.3 mo → 5.8–6.7 mo |
| B2 | 500–600 → 1,000–1,200 | 16.7–20 mo → 33–40 mo | 8.3–10 mo → 16.7–20 mo | 4.2–5 mo → 8.3–10 mo |
| C1 | 700–800 → 1,400–1,600 | 23–27 mo → 47–53 mo | 11.7–13.3 mo → 23–27 mo | 5.8–6.7 mo → 11.7–13.3 mo |
| C2 | 1,000–1,200 → 2,000–2,400 | 33–40 mo → 67–80 mo | 16.7–20 mo → 33–40 mo | 8.3–10 mo → 16.7–20 mo |

- Cross-check against FSI Cat III (~1,760 total h to ILR 3): about 59 months at 1 h/day, 29 months at 2 h/day, and 15 months at 4 h/day.
- Cross-check against Refold "other" (1,700 h functional fluency; ×1.5 = 2,550 h for "immigrate/university"): about 28 months to functional fluency at 2 h/day, and about 42 months for the immigration/university target.
- **For a typical Turkish adult who already tests A2 (school English):**
  - B2 needs about 300–420 more GLH, or about 600–840 total h. That is ~10–14 months at 2 h/day, or ~20–28 months at 1 h/day.
  - C1 needs about 500–620 more GLH, or ~1,000–1,240 total h. That is ~17–21 months at 2 h/day.
- **Adherence correction:** real adults rarely study 7 days/week. At 5 days/week, multiply months by 1.4.
- 4+ h/day is realistic only in intensive programs or between jobs. Practitioner sources suggest effectiveness drops beyond ~5 h/day.
- Living in the US adds unstructured exposure hours. These count only if comprehensible and attended to (see Q4, immersion).
- **Higher plateaus take longer:** each level up the scale needs more hours. Cambridge's GLH gaps grow from ~150–200 at lower levels to ~300–400 for C1→C2.

### Gaps
- No credible, validated "hours-to-CEFR calculator" was found. Online calculators (e.g., [hacecuentas](https://hacecuentas.com/en/duolingo-time-cefr-level-progress)) appear to repackage the same GLH figures and were not treated as evidence.
- No longitudinal study found that measures adult Turkish L1 learners' hours-to-level for English specifically.

## Q4. Comparing learning methods: apps, tutors, classes, intensive, immersion, AI

### Takeaway
- **Apps** (Duolingo, Babbel, busuu) have company-funded evidence of beginner-level gains, mostly in reading and listening, roughly to A2/low-B1. Speaking gains are partial, and dropout is high. Apps alone will not get an adult to B2/C1 for US work.
- **One-to-one tutoring** has the strongest general-education evidence (Bloom 1984: 2 SD; modern meta-analysis ~0.29–0.37 SD in K-12), though not specific to adult EFL.
- **AI conversation partners** show medium pooled effects (g ≈ 0.6) in 2024–2025 meta-analyses, mostly from short, small studies.
- **Immersion and study abroad** help mainly when learners actively interact. Time abroad alone is not a guarantee.

### Cited Findings
**Duolingo**
- Vesselinov & Grego (2012), commissioned by Duolingo: adult beginner Spanish learners gained "a semester's worth" in an average of ~34 hours. — [TechCrunch, 17 Jan 2013](https://techcrunch.com/2013/01/17/study-learning-spanish-with-duolingo-can-be-more-effective-than-college-classes-or-rosetta-stone/amp)
- Criticism: Krashen pointed to incomplete participation (per a search summary of his critique: of 156 volunteers, about 90 completed ~30 hours, and ~25% did ≤8 hours; numbers not independently verified). — [Krashen, "Research on Duolingo" (PDF)](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/research_on_duolingo.pdf)
- Broderick (Tokyo Denki University) found little effect of Duolingo on TOEIC scores of Japanese learners of English (search snippet only). — [Broderick, JALT CUE OCJSI Vol 2](https://jaltcue.org/files/OCJSI/OCJSI_Vol_2/OCJSI_2_pp28-34_Broderick.pdf)
- **English course study (Duolingo blog, Xiangying Jiang, 19 Sep 2022):**
  - 263 learners in the English-for-Spanish-speakers course, using Duolingo only, with no/very little prior English.
  - At completion of A2 content, ACTFL **Intermediate High in reading** and **Intermediate Mid in listening** (third-party assessment).
  - Scores at the end of A2 were significantly higher than at the end of A1.
  — [Duolingo Blog: Can You Learn English on Duolingo?](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-well-does-duolingo-teach-english/)
- Peer-reviewed version (CALICO Journal, 2024, Duolingo-affiliated authors): 245 English learners across three English courses. Learners at the end of A2 averaged Intermediate High in reading and listening. — [Avant Assessment PDF: Duolingo English Courses in Developing Proficiency](https://cdnprodwpv2.avantassessment.com/wp-content/uploads/Duolingo-English-Courses-in-Developing-Proficiency.pdf) (details via search summary)
- **Speaking (Duolingo blog, 1 Jun 2021):**
  - 258 learners who finished Unit 5 (end of A2) in Spanish/French took the Pearson Versant speaking test.
  - **66% of Spanish and 53% of French learners scored A2 or higher**; "about half" overall.
  - Subscores were in the A2 range except French pronunciation (lower).
  — [Duolingo Blog: How well does Duolingo teach speaking skills?](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-well-does-duolingo-teach-speaking-skills)
- **Intermediate (Duolingo blog, 20 Jan 2023):** 273 Spanish learners. End of A2 content ≈ 4 university semesters; halfway through B1 ≈ 5 semesters (reading and listening). — [Duolingo Blog: Duolingo teaches 5+ semesters](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-teaches-5-semesters)
- Third-party commentary (not research): LingQ argues most completers land around A2, and that completion rates at higher levels are "very low". — [LingQ blog](https://www.lingq.com/blog/can-you-become-fluent-with-duolingo/)

**Babbel**
- Loewen, Isbell & Sporn (2020, *Foreign Language Annals*; MSU press release 2019):
  - 85 MSU undergraduates used Babbel Spanish for 12 weeks; 54 completed.
  - **59% improved oral proficiency by ≥1 ACTFL sublevel** on the Oral Proficiency Interview. That rose to **69% for those with ≥6 hours** and **75% for those with ≥15 hours**.
  - Gains correlated with time in the app. **36% of starters quit.**
  — [MSU Today, 2019](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2019/new-study-gives-insight-on-effectiveness-of-language-learning-apps)
- The study had no control group and was funded by Babbel's parent, Lesson Nine GmbH. — [MedicalResearch.com interview](https://medicalresearch.com/can-you-really-learn-a-language-using-babbel/); Babbel's own release is promotional: [Babbel press release, 10 Jan 2019](https://uk.babbel.com/press/en-gb/releases/2019-01-10-Babbel-SPA-2018-Study.html)

**busuu**
- 2016 study led by Roumen Vesselinov: users needed on average ~22.5 hours to cover one college semester of Spanish. After ≥16 hours, 100% showed statistically significant improvement. Company-commissioned. — [McGraw-Hill Education press release, 7 Jun 2016](https://mheducation.mediaroom.com/2016-06-07-University-study-finds-measurable-improvement-of-language-skills-after-just-16-hours-of-learning-with-busuu)

**Self-study software in the workplace**
- Nielson (2011, *Language Learning & Technology* 15(3), pp. 110–129) studied US government employees using Rosetta Stone (150 absolute beginners: Arabic, Chinese, Spanish) and Tell Me More (176 US Coast Guard). She found "severe participant attrition", likely due to technical problems and "lack of sufficient support for autonomous learning". She concluded that "more resource-intensive types of language training are more likely to be effective." — [Nielson 2011, ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/handle/10125/44265)

**One-to-one tutoring**
- Bloom (1984, *Educational Researcher*): students tutored one-to-one with mastery learning performed ~2 SD above classroom students. "The average tutored student was above 98% of the students in the control class." The finding was based on two University of Chicago dissertations (Anania; Burke). — [Wikipedia: Bloom's 2 sigma problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_sigma_problem)
- Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (NBER w27476, Jul 2020), PreK–12 RCT meta-analysis: pooled effect **0.37 SD**. Effects were larger with teacher/paraprofessional tutors than with volunteers or parents. — [NBER w27476](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27476)
- A 2024 revised version reports a pooled effect of **0.288 SD**. — [Stanford NSSA](https://nssa.stanford.edu/studies/promise-tutoring-prek-12-learning-systematic-review-and-metaanalysis-experimental-evidence)

**Classes and intensive programs**
- The FSI model, the government's benchmark for fast adult results, is intensive: 23 classroom hours plus 17 self-study hours per week. — [state.gov](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/)

**Immersion and study abroad**
- Carroll's classic finding: "time spent abroad is clearly one of the most potent variables" predicting proficiency. — [ERIC EJ891446 (PDF)](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ891446.pdf)
- Brecht et al.: higher pre-departure reading/grammar scores predicted greater gains, suggesting a proficiency threshold for benefiting from study abroad. Other studies found lower-proficiency students gained more. — [ERIC EJ891446](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ891446.pdf); [Frontiers journal](https://frontiersjournal.org/index.php/Frontiers/article/download/62/45/97)
- Students "must actively immerse themselves in the target language… SA itself is not a guarantee for substantial language development." — [MDPI Languages 6(1):6, 2021](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-471X/6/1/6)
- Initial proficiency and language-use studies during short-term immersion (SSLA). — [Cambridge Core / SSLA](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/initial-proficiency-and-l2-grammar-development-during-shortterm-immersion-abroad/A573A756CB9F80398C1C5F4E67786E59)

**AI conversation partners (2024–2026 research)**
- Lyu, Lai & Guo (2025, *International Journal of Applied Linguistics*), meta-analysis of 31 studies (41 effect sizes):
  - Overall **g = 0.608** (medium).
  - Chatbots **with voice input g = 0.809 vs. 0.425** without.
  - GenAI-powered chatbots outperformed other types.
  — [Wiley IJAL 10.1111/ijal.12668](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668)
- "Unraveling Effects of AI Chatbots on EFL Learners' Language Skill Development: A Meta-analysis" (*Asia-Pacific Education Researcher*, 2024):
  - Overall **g = 0.648** [95% CI 0.406–0.891].
  - Text-based chatbots g = 1.075 vs. **voice-based g = 0.380**. This **contradicts** Lyu et al. on modality.
  - Intervention duration and interface design were significant moderators.
  — [Springer 10.1007/s40299-024-00853-2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40299-024-00853-2)
- A 2022–2024 EFL chatbot meta-analysis also exists. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/388829750_Can_AI_chatbots_effectively_improve_EFL_learners'_learning_effects-A_meta-analysis_of_empirical_research_from_2022-2024)
- *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* 12:1223 (2025), mixed methods:
  - 60 undergraduates in an IELTS prep center, assigned to Mondly AI conversation bot (6 weeks) or a control with alternative speaking practice.
  - Significant improvements in **IELTS Speaking** scores and reductions in speaking anxiety vs. control.
  — [Nature HSSC](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)
- ChatGPT Voice-specific studies are small and mostly uncontrolled:
  - Ecuador: 49 high-schoolers, 3 weeks, pre/post only, large gains in pronunciation, fluency, vocabulary and grammar. — [Ciencia Latina](https://ciencialatina.org/index.php/cienciala/article/download/16390/23425/)
  - 6-week ELSA Speak + ChatGPT Voice study: speaking rate rose from 92.4 to 118.4 words/min, and hesitation pauses fell from 12.8 to 6.2 per 100 words. Two tools combined, so the effects cannot be separated. — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/23323466)
  - Review of potentials and concerns (feedback accuracy, over-reliance). — [ResearchGate review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/383471345_Speaking_Practice_using_ChatGPT's_Voice_Conversation_A_Review_on_Potentials_and_Concerns)
- Search did not find any randomized controlled trial of ChatGPT voice mode specifically (as of Oct 2026).

### Inferences
- **Apps:** good for A0→A2 foundations (vocabulary, basic grammar, habit). The best company data shows only ~50–66% reaching A2 speaking at the end of A2 content, and nothing validated beyond mid-B1.
  - All app studies are company-funded or affiliated, tested only learners who persisted (survivorship bias), and mostly measured Spanish/French for English speakers.
  - Duolingo's English evidence is for Spanish speakers, not Turkish speakers.
- **Tutoring:** the 2 SD figure is an upper-bound ideal (two small dissertations, mastery learning, school subjects). Modern estimates are 0.29–0.37 SD in K-12.
  - For an adult, tutoring's main value is speaking and production time, individualized error correction, and accountability. These are exactly the skills Turkish learners score lowest on (EF EPI speaking 438).
- **AI partners:** a cheap, unlimited, low-anxiety speaking supplement, with medium effects in short studies. Evidence on voice vs. text is mixed. AI does not replace human interaction for pragmatics, accent comprehension or accountability.
- **Immersion/living in the US:** being physically in the US is not equivalent to learning. Gains depend on actual interaction (English-speaking work, friends, community). Arriving with B1–B2 likely lets one benefit more (the threshold hypothesis), though evidence is mixed.
- **Intensive programs:** the fastest route per hour of calendar time (FSI), but costly and incompatible with full-time work. Use them for short sprints (e.g., a 2–4 week intensive before relocation).
- **Group classes:** the comparison baseline in app studies is "university semesters", but no head-to-head adult EFL evidence was found. Group classes offer structure and accountability at lower cost than 1:1, with less speaking time per learner.

### Gaps
- No independent (non-company-funded) RCT of Duolingo, Babbel or busuu reaching B2+ was found.
- No efficacy research found for online tutoring marketplaces (italki, Preply, Cambly) for adult learners.
- No adult-EFL-specific meta-analysis of 1:1 tutoring vs. group classes found.
- No 2025–2026 RCT isolating ChatGPT / Gemini / Claude voice-mode effects on validated speaking tests (e.g., IELTS/TOEFL Speaking) over periods longer than ~6–12 weeks.
- Babbel's English-for-Turkish-speakers course has no published efficacy data found.
- Exact attrition percentages in Nielson (2011) could not be retrieved (PDF inaccessible).

## Q5. Famous self-taught and polyglot methods (practitioner opinion) and how they align with research

### Takeaway
Leading polyglot approaches converge on: (1) massive comprehensible input (listening and reading); (2) daily consistency over intensity; (3) a defined speaking stage. They disagree on when to speak (day one for Benny Lewis vs. after a large input base for Refold/Kaufmann). The input emphasis aligns with vocabulary-coverage research (98% coverage needs 6–9k word families). Their timelines are marketing-flavoured and assume a similar language.

### Cited Findings
- **Steve Kaufmann / LingQ:**
  - Kaufmann co-founded LingQ with his son Mark in 2007 around "massive listening and reading instead of flashcard drills", drawing on Krashen's input hypothesis. — [Built by Foundry profile](https://www.builtbyfoundry.io/blog/steve-kaufmann-lingq-language-learning-app)
  - He recommends focusing on input heavily early, after which "speaking takes care of itself", and reports listening to the same mini-story ~40 times. — [LearnEnglishPod](https://learnenglishpod.com/2023/10/11/how-steve-kaufmann-uses-comprehensible-input-to-learn-languages/)
  - LingQ's sample comprehensible-input routines:
    - 20-minute routine: 10 min listening, 5 min reading the transcript, 5 min vocabulary review.
    - Listening-focused routine: 20–30 min listening on a walk or commute plus 20–30 min re-listening with transcript.
    - Extensive reading: 20–60 min daily.
    — [LingQ: Comprehensible Input guide](https://www.lingq.com/blog/comprehensible-input-guide/) (company blog)
  - Critics on LingQ's forum call the approach "a very vague method". — [LingQ forum](https://forum.lingq.com/t/comprehensible-input-part-23/71364)
- **Refold (immersion-based "mass immersion" descendant):** a phased roadmap. — [Refold Roadmap](https://refold.la/roadmap/)
  - The phases are 0 Immersion Learning, 1 Foundations, 2 Comprehension ("grow your vocabulary and read alongside audio"), 3 Listening, 4 Speaking ("build the brain-to-mouth connection"), 5 Accuracy ("use writing to clean up grammar"), 6 Fluency, and 7 Beyond.
  - Hours for an "other" language: Foundations 100, Comprehension +400 (500 cumulative), Listening +400 (900), Speaking +200 (1,100). Functional fluency is about 1,700 h. — [Refold: How Long](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language)
- **Luca Lampariello:**
  - Beginner stage uses Assimil "With Ease" dialogue courses (~100 dialogues with audio, translation and grammar notes) plus Innovative Languages "Pod101".
  - Goal of "B1 in three months", "only realistic if your target language is similar to your native language".
  — [Lampariello: How to Learn a Language Fast](https://www.lucalampariello.com/how-to-learn-a-language/) (practitioner and commercial; the page links to his programs)
- **Benny Lewis / Fluent in 3 Months:** marketing promises to "Have a 15-minute conversation in your new language after 90 days", with a money-back guarantee for the paid Bootcamp. — [Fluent in 3 Months site](https://www.fluentin3months.com/) (marketing claim; the "speak from day one" article URL returned 404)
- **Research alignment (vocabulary):** Nation (2006): if 98% coverage is needed for unassisted comprehension, about **8,000–9,000 word families** are needed for written text and **6,000–7,000** for spoken text. — [Nation 2006, "How large a vocabulary is needed for reading and listening?" (PDF)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Where research supports the polyglots:**
  - Input volume matters: vocabulary thresholds can only realistically be reached through extensive reading and listening plus spaced review.
  - Time-on-task predicts gains (Babbel/MSU correlation; Carroll on time abroad).
  - Consistency matters (see Q8).
- **Where they overreach:**
  - Promises like "fluent in 3 months" or "B1 in 3 months" ignore language distance. Their own authors (Refold, Lampariello) concede this.
  - Pure input-only approaches underweight output. For an adult who must interview, work and socialize in the US within a fixed time, deliberate speaking practice (tutors, AI, real interaction) should start early, around A2, rather than after 1,000 hours.
  - Turkish learners' measured weakness is speaking and writing (EF EPI 2025).
- **Practical synthesis for this user:**
  - Refold/Kaufmann-style input as the backbone, at 50–60% of time from B1 onwards.
  - SRS vocabulary.
  - Lampariello-style structured course at the start.
  - Benny-style early speaking, adapted as weekly tutor/AI conversation from A2.

### Gaps
- No primary source fetched for AJATT (the original "All Japanese All The Time" site); described only via its descendant, Refold.
- Benny Lewis's "speak from day one" methodology page returned 404; only marketing claims were captured.
- No peer-reviewed study evaluating Refold/LingQ/AJATT-style programs as wholes was found.
- Paul Nation's "Four Strands" (roughly equal time for meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused learning and fluency development) is widely cited but was **not verified** in this session.

## Q6. An evidence-informed weekly plan by stage (A1–A2, B1–B2, C1) and avoiding the intermediate plateau

### Takeaway
For a busy adult, a sustainable 8–14 h/week (≈1–2 h/day) plan is better than sporadic marathons. The input:output:vocab:grammar ratio should shift from structured course and SRS at A1–A2 to input-heavy plus deliberate speaking at B1–B2, then domain-specific output at C1. The "intermediate plateau" is usually caused by running out of high-frequency words, staying in comfortable structures, and too little comprehensible yet challenging input. It is fixed by deliberate vocabulary expansion, harder input, and feedback on output.

### Cited Findings
- Vocabulary coverage targets: ~6,000–7,000 word families for spoken text and ~8,000–9,000 for written text at 98% coverage. — [Nation 2006 (PDF)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf)
- Refold phase sequencing: Foundations → Comprehension (vocab plus reading with audio) → Listening → Speaking → Accuracy (writing to fix grammar) → Fluency. — [Refold Roadmap](https://refold.la/roadmap/)
- Refold: losing effectiveness beyond ~5 h/day; "focus on creating a language learning routine that you can maintain every day, without fail." — [Refold](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language)
- LingQ daily input routines of 20–60 min (see Q5). — [LingQ](https://www.lingq.com/blog/comprehensible-input-guide/)
- Voice-input chatbots: g = 0.809 in one meta-analysis vs. 0.425 without voice (contradicted by another meta-analysis). — [Lyu et al. 2025](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ijal.12668); [Springer 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40299-024-00853-2)
- **Plateau causes (mostly teacher/practitioner sources; no large empirical studies found):**
  - Adult L2 ability improves quickly at first and then much more slowly at intermediate level, "when the learner can no longer find enough comprehensible English" (citing Krashen 1985). — [ESL Pod](https://www.eslpod.com/research/intermediate-plateau/)
  - At intermediate stage "high-frequency vocabulary runs out… You're now learning words that might only appear once every few thousand words." — [How We Learn Languages (Substack)](https://howwelearnlanguages.substack.com/p/breaking-through-the-plateau-the)
  - Learners rely on comfortable structures, and "breaking through fossilized errors" is one of the hardest parts. — [TEFL Academy](https://www.theteflacademy.com/blog/the-intermediate-plateau)
  - Routine (citing Ericsson) is a major plateau cause, but plateaus "can be overcome under certain pedagogical procedures". — [ASUE 2022 (PDF)](https://asue.am/upload/files/science/banber/2022-year-5/9.pdf)
  - Skill growth increasingly relies on "unlearning". — [Scott Young](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/14804)
- Turkish learners: speaking 438 and writing 441, vs. reading 503 (EF EPI 2025). — [EF EPI Türkiye](https://www.ef.com/wwen/epi/regions/europe/turkey/)

### Inferences
The plans below are a synthesis by these notes, built from the cited findings. They are not taken from a single validated program. Assumed budget: 10–12 h/week, i.e. about 75–90 min on weekdays plus about 2 h on each weekend day.

**Stage 1: A1–A2 (≈0–200 GLH; ~4–6 months at this budget, or less if starting from school English)**
- Structured course and grammar: ~35%, ≈4 h/wk. One coursebook or app course (e.g., Duolingo/Babbel path) for sequencing, plus short grammar explanations in Turkish where helpful.
- Vocabulary with SRS (Anki-type): ~20%, ≈2–2.5 h/wk, at 15–20 min/day. Target the first ~2,000–3,000 most frequent word families.
- Easy comprehensible input: ~30%, ≈3–3.5 h/wk. Graded readers, learner podcasts, slow news, children's/learner YouTube, repeated listening (Kaufmann-style).
- Speaking and pronunciation: ~15%, ≈1.5–2 h/wk.
  - Daily 5–10 min shadowing.
  - One 30–45 min tutor session per week.
  - AI voice practice on survival scripts (doctor, bank, phone calls, job small talk).

**Stage 2: B1–B2 (≈200–600 GLH; the core stage for US work; ~10–20 months)**
- Input: ~50%, ≈5–6 h/wk. Podcasts and TV/YouTube with English subtitles; US-specific content (news, workplace shows, industry podcasts); extensive reading. Choose material at about 95–98% comprehension.
- Vocabulary: ~15%, ≈1.5–2 h/wk. SRS fed by "sentence mining" from input; collocations and phrasal verbs.
- Output with feedback: ~25–30%, ≈3 h/wk.
  - 2 × 45–60 min tutor or conversation-class sessions per week.
  - 10–15 min daily AI voice conversation.
  - 1–2 short writings per week (emails, LinkedIn posts) corrected by a tutor or AI.
- Targeted grammar and error log: ~5–10%. Fix recurring errors (articles, tense aspect, prepositions; typical Turkish-L1 problem areas, per general knowledge, not sourced here).

**Stage 3: C1 (≈600–800+ GLH; ~1–2 more years)**
- Input: ~45–50%. Authentic professional and academic material in one's own field: long-form podcasts, books, meetings, lectures.
- Output: ~35%. Presentations, mock interviews, debates, professional writing with feedback, accent and intonation coaching.
- Vocabulary and register: ~15%. Domain terminology, idioms, pragmatic softeners, US cultural references.

**Anti-plateau tactics (B1→B2→C1), derived from the causes above:**
- Deliberately raise input difficulty every 6–8 weeks: move from learner content to native content and from familiar topics to unfamiliar ones.
- Mine low-frequency but useful words into SRS. Since high-frequency words run out, vocabulary growth must become intentional.
- Maintain an error log reviewed with a tutor to attack fossilized errors.
- Introduce new output genres (phone calls, presentations, negotiation role-plays).
- Retest every ~100–150 hours (see Q7) to make slow progress visible.

**General rules:**
- Daily minimum ("never zero"): even 15 min of SRS plus listening on bad days.
- Stack habits onto fixed cues: commute = listening, lunch = SRS, evening = tutor/AI.

### Gaps
- No empirical study was found that directly tests optimal input:output:vocab:grammar time ratios for adult EFL learners. The ratios above are a reasoned synthesis.
- Spaced-repetition efficacy (e.g., Cepeda et al. meta-analyses) and Nation's Four Strands were not verified in this session. They should be cited cautiously if used.
- Evidence on the intermediate plateau is mostly practitioner/teacher writing. No large-scale empirical studies were found.
- Turkish-L1-specific error patterns for English (articles, word order, th/w, vowel length) were not sourced from a contrastive-linguistics study here.

## Q7. How to self-assess level: free placement tests and how often to test

### Takeaway
Use the free EF SET (50-min reading/listening or 90-min four-skills, CEFR-aligned, scored 1–100) as the main free benchmark. The British Council EnglishScore app (~40 min, A2–C1, 0–599) is a second free check. Take an official test (IELTS/TOEFL/Cambridge) only when a score is needed for visas, jobs or universities. Retest roughly every 2–3 months, or every ~100–150 study hours.

### Cited Findings
- EF SET is free and offers two versions:
  - **50-minute** (reading and listening only).
  - **90-minute** (reading, writing, listening, speaking).
  - Both are "fully aligned with CEFR levels", with an optional certificate for CV/LinkedIn and a 20-question practice test.
  — [EF SET: CEFR](https://www.efset.org/cefr/)
- EF SET score bands: A1 1–30, A2 31–40, B1 41–50, B2 51–60, C1 61–70, C2 71–100 (overview page). The A1 page shows "21–30" (minor inconsistency on EF's site). — [EF SET overview](https://www.efset.org/cefr/); [EF SET A1](https://www.efset.org/cefr/a1/)
- EF (a test vendor) says "the best way to find out your CEFR level is to take a well-designed standardized test". On CVs, include a level descriptor, a standardized score and examples of use, because the CEFR is less understood in corporate settings. — [EF SET](https://www.efset.org/cefr/)
- British Council EnglishScore:
  - Mobile app test of ~40 min covering grammar, vocabulary, reading and listening.
  - CEFR result from **A2 to C1** plus a 0–599 score.
  - The test is free; a certificate costs money (from ~US$20).
  - It "cannot be used as a replacement for the IELTS test, Cambridge exams or a British Council Placement test".
  — [British Council EnglishScore](https://www.britishcouncil.org/english/learn-online/englishscore); [British Council Portugal](https://www.britishcouncil.pt/en/english/courses-online/englishscore); [British Council Türkiye](https://www.britishcouncil.org.tr/en/english/englishscore)
- Cambridge: approximately 200 guided hours per CEFR level. — [Cambridge English Support](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506)

### Inferences
- **Testing frequency:** at ~200 GLH per level, a learner studying 1–2 h/day moves about one half-level every ~3–4 months. Testing more often than every ~6–8 weeks mostly measures noise. Suggested cadence:
  - Baseline at start.
  - Then every ~100–150 hours (≈2–3 months).
  - One official mock (IELTS/TOEFL practice) about 3 months before any real exam.
- Use a four-skill test (EF SET 90-min), because receptive-only tests overestimate Turkish learners' speaking and writing (EF EPI skill gap).
- Supplement scores with CEFR can-do self-checks, e.g., "Can I follow a work meeting? Make a phone call to a US insurance company?"
- For US goals, the decisive tests are usually:
  - IELTS/TOEFL: university, some professional licensing.
  - Employer interviews: actual job readiness.

### Gaps
- The Cambridge English free online "Test your English" and the British Council 5-minute web test could not be verified (Cloudflare blocks, and search returned only a staging page).
- No independent validation study of EF SET or EnglishScore against IELTS/TOEFL was retrieved in this session.
- No research found on optimal retesting frequency for adult self-learners.

## Q8. Why adults fail or quit, and how to prevent it (habit formation, consistency)

### Takeaway
Attrition is the dominant failure mode: 36% quit in the Babbel/MSU study, "severe attrition" in workplace self-study software (Nielson 2011), and app efficacy data only covers persisters. Habit research (Lally et al. 2010) shows automaticity for a daily behavior takes on average ~66 days (range 18–254). A missed day does not derail it. Specific when-and-where plans increase follow-through.

### Cited Findings
- **Lally et al. (2010, *European Journal of Social Psychology*):**
  - Average time to form a daily habit was **66 days, range 18–254 days**. "What we really showed was that how long habit formation takes is highly variable."
  - Lally: "if you make a clear plan of when and where you will do something you are more likely to do it than if you have a vague intention." Also: "knowledge alone does not lead to behaviour change."
  — [University of Surrey interview with Dr Pippa Lally, 21 Jan 2026](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)
- Study design: 96 volunteers, a daily health behavior in the same context for 84 days. Missing one opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. Critique: the 66-day figure comes from the subset (reported as 39) with good model fits, and some modeled times exceed the 84-day observation window. — [The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit) (secondary analysis)
- **Babbel/MSU:** "36% of participants who started the study ended up quitting." Gains correlated with time invested. "What might be most important is that learners stick with it." — [MSU Today 2019](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2019/new-study-gives-insight-on-effectiveness-of-language-learning-apps)
- **Nielson (2011):** "severe participant attrition… likely due to a variety of technological problems as well as the lack of sufficient support for autonomous learning in the workplace". — [ScholarSpace](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/handle/10125/44265)
- A later critique quotes a "precipitous drop-off once the study was underway". The Rosetta Stone protocol required 10 h/week for 20 weeks. — [McQuillan 2019 "And then there were none" (PDF)](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/and_then_there_were_none_mcquillan_2019.pdf)
- In the Duolingo critique, a meaningful share of volunteers did not reach the target hours (per summary of Krashen; not independently verified). — [Krashen PDF](https://mail.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/research_on_duolingo.pdf)
- Duolingo's own framing: "Language learning is all about sticking with it! It takes a long time to build up proficiency." — [Duolingo Blog, 20 Jan 2023](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-teaches-5-semesters)
- Refold (practitioner): "Language learning is not a sprint… Focus on creating a language learning routine that you can maintain every day, without fail." It also sets a nearer "fun threshold" goal (~340 h for an "other" language) instead of the distant total. — [Refold](https://refold.la/roadmap/library/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language)
- Speaking anxiety is a known barrier. AI conversation bots reduced foreign-language speaking anxiety vs. control in one 2025 controlled study (n = 60). — [Nature HSSC 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-025-05550-z)

### Inferences
**Main failure reasons for adults:**
1. Unrealistic expectations, e.g., "fluent in 3 months" marketing vs. the 1,000–1,800 h reality for a Turkish speaker to reach professional level.
2. No structure or accountability in pure self-study (Nielson).
3. The intermediate plateau, which feels like no progress.
4. Work and family time pressure.
5. Speaking anxiety, which leads to avoiding output.
6. Tool-hopping instead of accumulating hours.

**Prevention:**
- Commit to the first ~66–90 days as habit-building, with fixed time and place (implementation intentions per Lally).
- Set intermediate milestones:
  - "Fun threshold" ≈ 300–350 h.
  - Each CEFR half-level.
  - Retests every 2–3 months.
- Add external accountability: a paid tutor at fixed weekly slots, a class, or a study partner. This directly counters the lack of support Nielson identified.
- Use a "never zero" minimum day; a missed day doesn't break habit formation (Lally).
- Track hours. Time-on-task predicts gains (MSU), and visible hours counter plateau discouragement.
- Use AI voice practice for low-stakes speaking to reduce anxiety before human conversations.
- Tie the study to a concrete US goal (job interview date, IELTS date, relocation date).

### Gaps
- Exact completion/attrition percentages for Nielson (2011) and for Duolingo's general user base were not retrieved.
- No study found specifically on dropout among adult Turkish learners of English.
- The "66 days" figure comes from health behaviors (eating, drinking, exercise), not language study. Transfer to a 30–120 min daily study session is an assumption.
