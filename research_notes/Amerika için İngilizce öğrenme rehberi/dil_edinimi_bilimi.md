## 1. Main SLA theories: what they claim, how strong the evidence is, and where there is consensus

### Takeaway
There is broad consensus that input is necessary but not sufficient. Comprehensible input is the fuel. Learning becomes faster and more accurate when it is combined with attention to form (noticing), pushed output, interaction with feedback, and some explicit instruction followed by practice that leads to automatization. Krashen's strong claim, that input alone is enough and that taught "learning" cannot become "acquisition", is not supported by the meta-analytic evidence on explicit instruction and corrective feedback (see Section 5).

### Cited Findings
- **Krashen's Input Hypothesis / comprehensible input (CI).** Critics include McLaughlin (1987), who said Krashen "does not provide evidence in any real sense of the term"; Gregg (1984), who said the theory "bypasses counter-evidence"; and Brown (2000), who called it "oversimplified" with "overstated" claims. These quotations come from a secondary podcast summary **[WEAK, secondary]** — [World Language Classroom podcast summary](https://music.amazon.com/podcasts/eaf574af-fc9f-4f6b-83cd-d689f96beb13/episodes/3506f0ed-038f-473c-b2e7-d681f33b01c9/world-language-classroom-the-origins-of-ci-krashen's-input-hypothesis)
- Critics also say Krashen's acquisition–learning distinction is defined too vaguely to be tested, and that the "affective filter" claim about children is contested because children also differ in motivation, confidence and anxiety — [Course handout, Univ. M'sila](https://elearning.univ-msila.dz/moodle/mod/resource/view.php?id=52532); [Course handout 2](https://elearning.univ-msila.dz/moodle/mod/resource/view.php?id=78752)
- Lichtman & VanPatten (2021), "Was Krashen right?", argue that the CI concept "has survived for forty years" under different names and is still driving SLA research, often without acknowledgment and under new terminology — [ICLA 2022 proceedings paper citing them](https://download.atlantis-press.com/proceedings/icla-22/125993063)
- Krashen (2017) himself acknowledges that the idea of communicative input predates him (citing VanPatten: Krashen "popularized" it) — [Krashen 2017, sdkrashen.com](https://www.sdkrashen.com/content/articles/2017_who_invented_compehensible_input.pdf)
- **Swain's Output Hypothesis (1985).** It came out of Canadian French immersion research: after years of comprehensible input, students' production still had grammatical errors. Swain argued that "comprehension of a message can take place with little syntactic analysis of the input, [but] production forces learners to pay attention to morphosyntax." She later proposed three functions of output: (1) noticing, (2) forming and testing hypotheses, (3) metalinguistic reflection — [Overview, Tohoku Univ.](https://www.ihe.tohoku.ac.jp/CPD/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/pdb_v7_12.pdf); [Overview, Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125973657.pdf)
- **Long's Interaction Hypothesis (1983; updated 1996).** Negotiation of meaning makes input comprehensible, and interaction "connects input, internal learner capacities, particularly selective attention, and output in productive ways." The 1996 version builds on Schmidt and Swain — [Cambridge, "Interaction as practice"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/practice-in-a-second-language/interaction-as-practice/6A969C78ED81B65935D1223207B322AE); [overview](https://www.ihe.tohoku.ac.jp/CPD/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/pdb_v7_12.pdf)
- **Interaction evidence [STRONG].** Mackey & Goo (2007), a meta-analysis cited in Lyster & Saito (2010), found that corrective feedback given during L2 interaction had a medium effect on immediate post-tests (d ≈ 0.71) and a large effect on delayed post-tests (d ≈ 1.09) (secondary) — [Lyster & Saito 2010, McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/6w924g64b)
- **Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis (1990; idea first in 1983).** "Mere exposure to input may not be beneficial unless 'noticing' occurs." In its strong form, attention is the necessary and sufficient condition for input to become intake. Note that the strong claim that subconscious learning is impossible is debated — [Overview sources](https://www.ihe.tohoku.ac.jp/CPD/wp/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/pdb_v7_12.pdf); [hispadoc](https://hispadoc.es/descarga/articulo/9901560.pdf)
- **Skill Acquisition Theory (DeKeyser, 2007).** Learning moves from declarative knowledge (explicit rules), to procedural knowledge (smooth execution), to automatic knowledge through consistent practice. The narrow claim that a skill is acquired only through direct repeated practice of that skill has "less support". However, studies show acquisition may stay incomplete without opportunities to "notice the gap" — [Overview](https://so17.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JEL/article/download/116/83/310); [Atlantis Press](https://www.atlantis-press.com/article/125973657.pdf)

### Inferences
- In practice the theories complement one another more than they compete. A sound adult regime is: (a) large amounts of comprehensible input (reading and listening), plus (b) deliberate attention to form and some explicit grammar, plus (c) speaking and writing output with feedback, plus (d) repeated practice until retrieval is automatic. This matches what Lightbown & Spada (*How Languages Are Learned*) present as the mainstream view, although I did not retrieve that book in this session.
- Online CI-only movements ("just watch content and grammar will come") take Krashen to an extreme. The meta-analyses in Section 5 show that explicit instruction and feedback give large, durable gains. Treat "never study grammar" as a popular claim, not a research consensus.

### Gaps
- Usage-based approaches (N. Ellis, Tomasello, Bybee: language learned as constructions through frequency and statistical learning) were not retrieved with a citable source in this session. I can say nothing sourced about them here.
- The exact Mackey & Goo (2007) effect sizes were not verified in the primary text. The figures above come from Lyster & Saito's citation of them.
- I did not access Lightbown & Spada (2013/2021) or Ellis's textbooks directly.

## 2. Memory science for durable learning: spacing, retrieval practice, interleaving, desirable difficulties, sleep, and SRS / FSRS

### Takeaway
The two most robust tools for long retention are **spacing** (spreading reviews over time, with longer gaps for longer retention goals) and **retrieval practice** (testing yourself rather than rereading). Both are supported by meta-analyses, and the first already has L2-specific meta-analytic support. Sleep between sessions consolidates vocabulary. Interleaving (mixing grammar structures) helps long-term retention of grammar. Modern SRS (Anki with FSRS) applies spacing and retrieval automatically and predicts forgetting better than Anki's old SM-2 algorithm.

### Cited Findings
- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008), *Psychological Science* [STRONG, large N].** More than 1,350 participants learned facts, reviewed them after a gap of up to 3.5 months, and were tested after a further delay of up to 1 year. Performance first rose, then fell, as the gap increased. The optimal gap grew as the test delay grew. As a share of the test delay, the optimal gap shrank from about **20–40% of a 1-week retention interval** to about **5–10% of a 1-year retention interval**. The authors concluded that "many educational practices are highly inefficient" — [PubMed 19076480](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/); [PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- **Kim & Webb (2022), "The effects of spaced practice on second language learning: A meta-analysis," *Language Learning* 72(1), 269–319 [STRONG].** A narrative review describes it as covering 37 experimental studies. It confirms that spacing has a positive role in L2 learning, with different effects by L2 area, learner and activity — [Review, SSLLT](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/download/35602/30649/76142)
- Kim's related doctoral meta-analysis (Western University) covered 98 effect sizes from 48 experiments (N = 3,411). Spacing had a **medium-to-large effect** on L2 learning. Shorter spacing was as effective as longer spacing on immediate post-tests but **less effective on delayed post-tests**. **Equal and expanding spacing schedules were statistically equivalent** — [Kim thesis, Western Univ.](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/)
- **Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Psychological Science* [STRONG, widely replicated].** Figures verified from the original PDF:
  - Experiment 1 (study+test vs. study+restudy). After 5 minutes, restudy was better (81% vs. 75%). **After 2 days, testing was better (68% vs. 54%, d = 0.95). After 1 week, testing was better (56% vs. 42%, d = 0.83).**
  - Experiment 2. At 1 week, repeated testing (STTT) recalled **61%**, SSST 56% and repeated study (SSSS) **40%**. Over the week, SSSS forgot **52%** of what it had recalled initially, SSST 28% and STTT only **14%**.
  - Repeated studying made students *more confident* that they would remember, even though they remembered less. This is the classic "illusion of fluency" behind Bjork's desirable difficulties — [Roediger & Karpicke 2006 PDF](https://psychology.ecu.edu/wp-content/pv-uploads/sites/216/2019/03/Roediger-Karpicke-2006.pdf)
- **Sleep: Mazza et al. (2016), "Relearn Faster and Retain Longer: Along With Practice, Sleep Makes Perfect," *Psychological Science* [MODERATE, N = 40].** Participants learned foreign vocabulary in two sessions 12 hours apart. The sleep group learned in the evening and relearned after sleeping; the wake group learned in the morning and relearned that evening. The sleep group needed fewer trials and less time to relearn all 16 words. At 1 week they recalled about 15 pairs versus about 11. The advantage persisted at 6 months. Popular summaries say "remember twice as much at 6 months"; I did not verify that figure in the paper — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797616659930/); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2016/9/15-1); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2016/08/160822083446.htm)
  - Caveat: the original used intensive relearning to a criterion of all 16 correct in a row. A replication with a less demanding criterion was in progress, and I did not find its results — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02487151)
- **Interleaving in L2 grammar: Nakata & Suzuki (2019), "Mixing grammar exercises facilitates long-term retention: Effects of blocking, interleaving, and increasing practice," *Modern Language Journal* 103(3), 629–647 [MODERATE].** Cited in later work as evidence that interleaved schedules benefit L2 grammar learning. Other evidence suggests **blocked** practice may benefit **pronunciation** learning — [Paris Cité seminar abstract](https://sciences-du-langage.univ-tlse2.fr/accueil/manifestations/seminaire-lnpl-interleaving-for-l2-grammar-learning-is-mixing-structures-beneficial-maud-pelissier-altae-urp-3967-universite-paris-cite); [Univ. Barcelona study](https://diposit.ub.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/911f8da8-9666-4917-9411-c9ae6754cd7b/content)
- **SRS: Anki and FSRS.**
  - FSRS's default parameters are fit to about 727 million reviews from about 10,000 users, and FSRS predicts recall better than SM-2. FSRS is opt-in in Anki, and Anki's default remains SM-2-derived — [Wikipedia: Anki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anki)
  - In the open SRS benchmark (9,999 collections, about 350 million evaluation reviews), FSRS-6 has lower log loss than Anki SM-2 for **99.6% of users**. The author warns that SM-2 was not designed to output recall probabilities, so "there is no way to have a truly fair, no caveats, comparison." FSRS-6 beat FSRS-5 (log loss 0.3460 vs. 0.3561 without same-day reviews) — [Expertium benchmark](https://expertium.github.io/Benchmark.html); [open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/srs-benchmark)
  - Claims that FSRS cuts reviews by about 20–30% at equal retention appear only in secondary or marketing sources. Some users report more reviews at first **[WEAK]** — [AnkiWeb forum](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/why-does-fsrs-provide-fewer-review-than-the-original-anki-sm-2/35567); [Diane guide](https://www.diane.app/en/guides/fsrs-vs-sm2)

### Inferences
- Applied to vocabulary: put new words or phrases (ideally example sentences, recognition and production cards) into Anki with FSRS enabled. A desired retention of about 0.85–0.90 is a common default; I did not source this setting in this session. Always try to recall before revealing the answer. Do reviews daily, in short sessions.
- Applied to grammar: use mixed (interleaved) practice sets, for example past simple vs. present perfect vs. past continuous in one exercise, rather than 20 items of one tense. Review problem structures at expanding gaps.
- The Cepeda ridgeline means that for a goal like "still fluent in 1 year", reviews should eventually be spread weeks to a month or more apart. Cramming before an exam (IELTS/TOEFL) helps the test but not long-term use.
- Studying vocabulary in the evening and reviewing the next morning, after sleep, is a low-cost way to apply Mazza et al.
- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork):** practice that feels harder (retrieval, spacing, interleaving) tends to give better long-term retention, while easy rereading inflates confidence. Roediger & Karpicke's confidence data illustrate this.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sources for Ebbinghaus (1885) or Cepeda et al. (2006, *Psychological Bulletin* meta-analysis). Its commonly cited scope, 839 assessments from 317 experiments, is unverified here.
- I did not verify the effect sizes in practice-testing meta-analyses (e.g., Rowland 2014; Adesope et al. 2017).
- No peer-reviewed RCT was found comparing FSRS and SM-2 on actual language-learning outcomes; the evidence is prediction benchmarks.
- I did not retrieve Bjork's primary papers on "desirable difficulties".

## 3. Vocabulary: how many words, which lists, incidental vs. intentional learning, and how many encounters

### Takeaway
Coverage research says that to understand unsimplified English, a learner needs about **3,000 word families for basic (95%) coverage of speech and TV**, about **6,000–7,000 for comfortable listening (98%)** and about **8,000–9,000 for comfortable reading (98%)**. Word knowledge grows with repeated encounters. About 8–10+ meetings are typical for durable or rich knowledge, but no fixed magic number exists. The fastest route combines deliberate study of high-frequency words (SRS and lists such as the NGSL) with large amounts of reading and listening input.

### Cited Findings
- **Nation (2006), "How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?" *Canadian Modern Language Review* 63(1), 59–81 [STRONG, foundational].** If 98% coverage is needed for unassisted comprehension, a learner needs **8,000–9,000 word families for written text** and **6,000–7,000 for spoken text**. Genre estimates: newspapers 8,000–9,000; novels 9,000; children's movies 7,000; unscripted spoken English 6,000–7,000; an introductory economics textbook 9,000. For **95% coverage of spoken text, about 3,000 word families plus proper nouns** are needed — [Nation 2006 PDF (Victoria Univ. of Wellington)](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0018/1626120/2006-How-large-a-vocab.pdf); [Replication study, Language Teaching](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- **van Zeeland & Schmitt (2012):** 95% coverage may be enough for listening comprehension, reachable with **2,000–3,000 word families** — [Nottingham eprints, replication](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/32292/)
- TOEFL iBT listening: 3,000 families plus proper nouns and marginal words gave 95% coverage, and **6,000 families gave 98%** — [JALT TLT](https://jalt-publications.org/tlt/articles/4281-vocabulary-size-required-toefl-ibt-listening-section)
- **TV: Webb & Rodgers (2009), 88 TV programs.** About **3,000 word families plus proper nouns and marginal words = 95% coverage**; about **7,000 = 98%**. Children's programs need only 2,000 for 95%. News has the most low-frequency words. The authors argued that regular viewing could lead to large incidental vocabulary gains — [IJES review](https://revistas.um.es/ijes/article/download/137131/124431/522041); [TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1603/1365/3261)
- A 2022 study: **unscripted TV** reaches 95% coverage with the 2,000 most frequent word families, while **scripted TV** (series) needs 3,000 — [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.831684/full)
- **Frequency lists: New General Service List (NGSL).** Browne, Culligan & Phillips (2013; updated 2016 and 2023) list about **2,809 lemmas**. The developers report about **92% average coverage** of general English texts (v1.2, 2023) and 94% for TOEIC — [Wikipedia: NGSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_General_Service_List); [newgeneralservicelist.com](https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/new-general-service-list)
  - Independent checks vary by corpus. The NGSL covered 87.7% of a Japanese university corpus versus 79.7% for the old GSL. On a section of COCA, the NGSL gave 4.32 percentage points better coverage than the GSL (Stoeckel 2019) — [JALT 2018 proceedings](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jalt2018-pcp-024.pdf); [JALT NGSL coverage](https://jalt-publications.org/articles/26145-ngsl-coverage-shs-entrance-exam-and-national-center-test)
- **Number of encounters:**
  - **Webb (2007), *Applied Linguistics* 28, 46–65:** at least **10 encounters** were needed to approach "rich" knowledge. This used a demanding 10-test battery covering spelling, associations, grammar, form and meaning, receptive and productive — [JALT JER review](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/download/301/59/2397)
  - **Waring & Takaki (2003), *Reading in a Foreign Language* 15(2):** words met **at least 8 times** in a graded reader had about a **50% chance of being recognized 3 months later**. Immediately after reading, learners recognized the meaning of 42% of target words but could translate only 18%. After 3 months these fell to 24% and **4%** — [NFLRC/RFL article](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/78); [JALT JER review](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/download/301/59/2397)
  - **Uchihara, Webb & Yanagisawa (2019), *Language Learning* 69(3), 559–599, meta-analysis [STRONG].** Repetition is positively related to incidental learning of single words; results for multiword units are mixed. The effect is **larger for university students** than for school-age learners. The number of repetitions needed across studies ranged **from 2 to 20**, with fewer needed by learners with larger vocabularies. Frequency "is an important predictor ... [but] not a single determiner" — [Uchihara et al. (Reading repository)](https://reading-clone.eprints-hosting.org/108688/8/13621688221140521.pdf); [todoele summary](https://todoele.net/node/429491)
  - The review literature stresses that "there is no particular minimum number of repetitions that ensures learning" — [JALT JER review](https://jalt-publications.org/content/index.php/jer/article/download/301/59/2397)

### Inferences
- Practical roadmap for a Turkish adult moving to the US:
  - Learn the roughly 2,800 NGSL words, or an equivalent of about 3,000 high-frequency families, deliberately with SRS. This gives about 90%+ text coverage and about 95% of everyday speech and TV with proper nouns.
  - Then grow toward 6,000–7,000 families, enough for 98% of conversation and TV, mostly through massive listening and reading, plus SRS for useful words met in context.
  - 8,000–9,000 is the target for comfortable reading of novels and newspapers.
- Incidental learning alone is slow. Waring & Takaki's 4% translation retention after 3 months shows this. Deliberate study (SRS) of words first met in input dramatically raises retention. Uchihara et al.'s finding that adults benefit more from repetition favours adults doing a lot of input.
- Plain incidental exposure is weakest for multiword units (phrasal verbs, collocations). These deserve explicit study, which is especially relevant for Turkish speakers (see Section 9).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sources for the Oxford 3000/5000 (CEFR-tagged lists) or COCA frequency lists. Their CEFR mappings are unverified here.
- Nation's commonly cited "1,000 words per year" incidental-growth estimates, and the Hu & Nation (2000) 98% reading-comprehension study, were not retrieved.

## 4. Extensive reading, extensive listening, and watching TV/series with subtitles

### Takeaway
Extensive reading has meta-analytic support: a small-to-medium effect on reading proficiency, and **larger for adults** (d ≈ 0.70). Captioned video (English audio with **English** subtitles) has a large effect on listening comprehension and vocabulary in meta-analysis (vocabulary g ≈ 0.87). Same-language captions support word-form learning and are generally at least as good as L1 (Turkish) subtitles. Per-episode vocabulary gains from TV are small, so the benefit comes from volume and regular viewing.

### Cited Findings
- **Jeon & Day (2016), *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2), meta-analysis of extensive reading [STRONG with caveats].** 71 samples from 49 studies (1980–2014), 5,919 participants. **d = 0.46** for group contrasts and **d = 0.71** for pre–post contrasts. By age: **adults d = 0.70**, children 0.52, adolescents 0.35. EFL settings > ESL settings. Web-based stories > paper books. ER integrated into the curriculum had the largest effect — [ScholarSpace (Univ. Hawaii)](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899); [NFLRC](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item-detail/354)
- Caveat: earlier ER research is criticized for weak control groups and flaws in measurement, design and analysis — [Frontiers in Psychology 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00617/full)
- **Montero Perez, Van Den Noortgate & Desmet (2013), "Captioned video for L2 listening and vocabulary learning: A meta-analysis," *System* 41(3) [STRONG].** It found "a clear and large effect of captions on both listening comprehension and vocabulary learning" (as summarized by Peters et al. 2016). For vocabulary, 10 studies gave **g = .87** (large). Moderators were under-reported — [Peters et al. 2016 PDF](https://betatest.planetread.org/pdf/Learning_vocabulary_through_audiovisual_input_The_differential_effect_of_L1_subtitles_and_captions.pdf); [MDPI review](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7102/13/3/274/htm)
- **Peters, Heynen & Puimège (2016), *System* 63, 134–148 [MODERATE].** Two exploratory studies with Flemish EFL learners (intermediate general-school and low-proficiency vocational). "Although learning gains were generally low," **captions (L2 subtitles) increased form learning**. Learners who watched with **L1 subtitles did not outperform the captions group on meaning tests**. Learners' vocabulary size and how often a word occurred in the video both correlated positively with learning. The odds of a correct form-recognition answer rose **about 10% per additional occurrence** — [Peters, Heynen & Puimège 2016 PDF](https://betatest.planetread.org/pdf/Learning_vocabulary_through_audiovisual_input_The_differential_effect_of_L1_subtitles_and_captions.pdf)
  - Secondary summaries disagree about whether captions clearly beat L1 subtitles for meaning. The original says only that L1 subtitles were *not better* — [CALL-EJ summary](https://callej.org/index.php/journal/article/view/352); [TESL Ontario symposium writeup](https://contact.teslontario.org/lessons-from-westerns-symposium-on-teaching-and-learning-vocabulary-in-another-language/)
- Audiovisual input is harder for word learning than reading because "learners cannot go back to a previous word or sentence", which makes guessing harder — [Peters et al. 2016](https://betatest.planetread.org/pdf/Learning_vocabulary_through_audiovisual_input_The_differential_effect_of_L1_subtitles_and_captions.pdf)
- Webb & Rodgers: regular TV viewing could produce large incidental gains over time (see Section 3) — [TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/1603/1365/3261)

### Inferences
- Recommended progression for a Turkish learner:
  - Graded readers or level-appropriate texts at about 98% known words for fluent extensive reading.
  - Series with **English subtitles**, not Turkish ones. The meta-analytic evidence is for same-language captions, and they directly train Turkish speakers' weak link between English spelling and sound.
  - Gradually move to no subtitles for familiar shows.
  - Start with unscripted or children's content (2,000-family load) before dense scripted dramas and news.
- Rewatching the same episode or series increases repeated encounters. Peters et al.'s frequency effect supports this.
- Adults gain the most from extensive reading (d = 0.70). This supports making extensive reading a core daily habit for adult learners.

### Gaps
- The Montero Perez (2013) listening-specific effect size was not verified; it is often quoted as about 0.99, but that is unconfirmed here.
- Primary papers by Day & Bamford (1998) and by Waring on extensive reading and extensive listening, and Peters & Webb (2018) on incidental TV learning, were not retrieved.
- Newer subtitle meta-analyses after 2020 were not retrieved.

## 5. Explicit grammar instruction and corrective feedback

### Takeaway
**[STRONG]** Explicit, focused instruction works, outperforms implicit instruction, and its effects are durable. This holds for both simple and complex structures and shows up on free, spontaneous production measures, not only on grammar tests. Corrective feedback has significant, durable effects. Recasts (the teacher simply reformulating) are the most common but least noticed type; prompts that make the learner self-correct produce more uptake.

### Cited Findings
- **Norris & Ortega (2000), "Effectiveness of L2 instruction: A research synthesis and quantitative meta-analysis," *Language Learning* 50, 417–528.** 49 studies (1980–1998). Focused L2 instruction gives large gains. **Explicit > implicit instruction.** Effects appear **durable**, persisting on delayed post-tests. Focus on Form and Focus on FormS were about equally effective — [Synapse summary](https://synapsesocial.com/papers/W2006181802); [Columbia TESOL Working Papers](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1538/588/3845); [Columbia (Gu 2007)](https://academiccommons.columbia.edu/doi/10.7916/j9b8-qj87)
  - Caveat noted by Norris & Ortega themselves: outcome measures "typically ... seem to favor more explicit types of treatments" — [CJAL 2022 article](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/CJAL-2022-0208/html)
- **Spada & Tomita (2010), *Language Learning* 60(2), 263–308, meta-analysis of 41 studies.** Explicit instruction had larger effects than implicit instruction for **both simple and complex features**. It contributed to both controlled knowledge and **spontaneous use** (free constructed responses), and gains held on delayed post-tests — [CASLS InterCom](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/23177); [Univ. Toronto record](https://www.eas.utoronto.ca/node/790)
- **Goo, Granena, Yilmaz & Novella (2015), "Norris & Ortega (2000) revisited and updated."** 34 studies directly comparing explicit and implicit instruction (11 from N&O plus 23 from 1999–2011). Explicit instruction again came out ahead — [John Benjamins](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/sibil.48.18goo/html)
- **Lyster & Ranta (1997), *SSLA* 19(1), 37–66.** 18.3 hours of four French immersion classrooms. Teacher feedback types: **recasts 55%**, elicitation 14%, clarification requests 11%, others < 10% each. **Recasts had the lowest uptake (about 30–31%)**. Elicitation, clarification requests, metalinguistic clues and repetition led more often to learner self-repair. Students may not recognize recasts as corrections — [McGill eScholarship](https://escholarship.mcgill.ca/downloads/0p096b851); [Univ. Bergamo PDF](https://aisberg.unibg.it/retrieve/handle/10446/121/1032/Lef23(2006)Jimenez.pdf)
- **Lyster & Saito (2010), "Oral feedback in classroom SLA," *SSLA* 32, 265–302, meta-analysis of 15 classroom studies (N = 827).** Corrective feedback had **significant and durable effects** on target-language development — [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/oral-feedback-in-classroom-sla/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- **Li (2010), "The effectiveness of corrective feedback in SLA: A meta-analysis," *Language Learning* 60, 309–365.** Overall **medium effect** for feedback, per an annotated bibliography — [Springer reference entry](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-030-79143-8_63); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/4999EE1C8379B2BF026B148EAF373CA1)
- Relative clauses for Turkish learners: a 2020 Hacettepe study found that **explicit instruction "facilitated the acquisition of relative clauses to a great extent"** — [TR Dizin](https://search.trdizin.gov.tr/yayin/detay/395241)

### Inferences
- For an adult Turkish learner: a short, clear explanation of a structure, especially the L1-transfer trouble spots (articles, present perfect, prepositions), followed by plenty of meaningful practice and input, is evidence-based. Grammar-only teaching without communication (the typical Turkish school model; see Section 10) is a separate problem. The problem is the *lack of output and input*, not explicit grammar itself.
- When speaking with tutors or partners in the US, ask explicitly for corrections and prompts ("tell me when I make a mistake; let me try to fix it") rather than relying on recasts, which often go unnoticed.

### Gaps
- Exact pooled effect sizes were not verified from primary texts:
  - Norris & Ortega overall instruction effect (commonly cited d ≈ 0.96, explicit ≈ 1.13 vs. implicit ≈ 0.54)
  - Li (2010) overall d ≈ 0.61
  - Spada & Tomita explicit vs. implicit d values
  - Goo et al. (2015) pooled values

  The commonly cited values above are flagged as unverified.

## 6. Age effects: the Critical Period Hypothesis and whether adults can reach high proficiency

### Takeaway
The largest study to date, Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018), finds that grammar-learning ability stays high until about **17.4 years** and then declines steadily. Adults rarely reach native-speaker-identical *grammar* if they start late, but they can reach high proficiency. Full attainment takes many years (about 30 in immersion). Adults have advantages in speed, explicit learning and benefit from repetition and extensive reading.

### Cited Findings
- **Hartshorne, Tenenbaum & Pinker (2018), "A critical period for second language acquisition: Evidence from 2/3 million English speakers," *Cognition* 177, 263–277.**
  - 669,498 participants took an online grammar quiz. Grammar-learning ability is preserved "almost to the crux of adulthood" (**17.4 years**) and then declines steadily.
  - Both native and non-native speakers took about **30 years to reach asymptotic performance**, at least in immersion settings.
  - Learners who started **by about 10–12 years** reached native-bilingual-like final attainment. After that, attainment fell continuously with no clear floor at puberty.
  - Immersion learners reached a higher ceiling than non-immersion learners.
  - Late learners often surpassed young native speakers but tended not to match mature native speakers, partly from "running out of time" before ability declines.

  [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6559801); [MIT DSpace](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/126641)
- Limitations of the study:
  - It tested **written grammar (syntax) only**, not vocabulary, accent, listening or speaking. The critical period may differ for those.
  - Participants were self-selected online volunteers.
  - It analysed 95 of 132 items.
  - It did not establish *why* ability declines (social changes at around 17–18, L1 interference and neural maturation are all possible).

  [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6559801)
- **Alternative interpretation:** Hernandez et al. (2020, *Cognition*) read the same data as consistent with emergentist models, in which brain and behaviour reorganize together, rather than a hard-wired biological cut-off — [PMC7736136](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7736136)
- **Adults' learning advantages:**
  - Extensive reading had its **largest effect for adults (d = 0.70)** versus children (0.52) and adolescents (0.35) — [Jeon & Day 2016](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899)
  - The effect of repetition on incidental vocabulary learning was **larger for university students** than for school learners — [Uchihara et al. 2019](https://todoele.net/node/429491)

### Inferences
- For a Turkish adult moving to the US, the realistic goal is high functional proficiency (CEFR B2–C1/C2, fluent professional communication), not being indistinguishable from a native speaker. Hartshorne et al. show that learning continues for decades in immersion, so moving to the US is a long-term accelerator *if* it brings real English input and interaction (see Section 8).
- "Adults can't learn languages" is a myth. "Adults rarely reach native-identical grammar and accent" is the evidence-based version.

### Gaps
- Primary adult-advantage studies were not retrieved: the rate-advantage literature (e.g., Snow & Hoefnagel-Höhle 1978) and pronunciation and age studies (e.g., Flege). Accent-specific age effects are not covered by a source here.

## 7. Motivation, habit, time, and anxiety

### Takeaway
A vivid "ideal L2 self" strongly predicts *intended effort* (r ≈ .61) but only weakly predicts *actual achievement* (r ≈ .20). Motivation must therefore be turned into concrete, regular practice behaviour. Language anxiety has a reliable moderate negative relationship with achievement (r = −.36). Total study time is large: about 200 guided hours per CEFR level, and about 500–600 cumulative hours to reach B2. Spacing research favours consistent daily practice over occasional marathon sessions. "Learning styles" matching is a myth.

### Cited Findings
- **Al-Hoorie (2018), meta-analysis of Dörnyei's L2 Motivational Self System, *Studies in Second Language Learning and Teaching*.** 32 reports, 39 samples, **32,078 learners**. The **ideal L2 self correlates with intended effort at r = .61** but with **objective achievement at only r = .20**, with substantial heterogeneity. Part of the strong learning-experience–effort link is a measurement artefact from overlapping questionnaire wording — [SSLLT article](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ssllt/article/view/12295); [Georgetown repository](https://repository.digital.georgetown.edu/handle/10822/1064671)
  - A later dissertation covering 100 studies found ideal L2 self–intended effort r = .61 (ρ = .77) and ideal L2 self–achievement **r = .24** (ρ = .26), a small effect — [Georgetown repository](https://repository.digital.georgetown.edu/handle/10822/1064671)
- **Teimouri, Goetze & Plonsky (2019), "Second language anxiety and achievement: A meta-analysis," *SSLA* 41(2), 363–387 [STRONG].** 97 reports, 105 samples, **N = 19,933**, 23 countries, 216 effect sizes. **Mean r = −.36** between L2 anxiety and achievement. The effect is moderated by type of achievement measure, educational level, target language and type of anxiety — [NAU Experts](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/second-language-anxiety-and-achievement/); [Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/B140169CF7C0BB6F5CE7BB0D1F9CDFA0)
- **Time needed (Cambridge English guided learning hours, cumulative from beginner):**
  - **B1 ≈ 350–400 h**, **B2 ≈ 500–600 h**, **C1 ≈ 700–800 h**
  - About **200 guided hours per CEFR level**
  - "a guideline only", depending on background and intensity

  [Cambridge English Helpdesk](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours)
- **Consistency over intensity:** spaced practice beats massed practice in L2 learning (medium-to-large effect), and longer spacing is better on delayed tests — [Kim thesis/meta-analysis](https://ir.lib.uwo.ca/etd/8600/); [Cepeda et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19076480/)
- **[MYTH] Learning styles (visual/auditory/kinesthetic "meshing").** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork (2008), *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9(3), 105–119. Studies with the right design (measuring styles, random assignment, same test for everyone) were rare, and those that exist "provide no evidence" that matching instruction to learning style improves achievement. The authors left open that a future experiment could show a benefit — [USF Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1765/); [Texas LD Center summary](https://texasldcenter.org/?p=355)

### Inferences
- **[MYTH] "Fluent in 30 days" or "learn English in 3 months" claims.** Cambridge's hour estimates (about 500–600 h to B2) and Hartshorne's decades-long attainment curve make such claims implausible for real fluency. At 1 hour/day, B2 from zero is roughly 1.5 years. At 3 hours/day, about 6 months. A Turkish school graduate who already has partial A2/B1 knowledge starts further along.
- Turn motivation into habit: since the ideal self predicts *intended* effort far better than achievement, a vivid "future me in the US" vision should be paired with fixed daily routines (SRS reviews, listening, speaking sessions) and tracked hours.
- Anxiety reduction matters for Turkish learners, who often fear making mistakes (see Section 10). Low-stakes speaking (tutors, language exchanges, self-talk, shadowing) before high-stakes situations is reasonable given r = −.36.

### Gaps
- No source was retrieved on Horwitz, Horwitz & Cope (1986), the FLCAS scale, or on optimal daily minutes of study. Daily-time recommendations here are derived from the GLH figures, not from a study.
- The FSI difficulty ratings (for English speakers learning other languages) were not retrieved. They don't map directly onto Turkish speakers learning English.

## 8. Immersion vs. classroom: study-abroad research, and whether living in the US guarantees fluency

### Takeaway
Living abroad helps, but **gains depend on how much the learner actually uses English** (social networks, contact) and on **starting proficiency**. Duration matters: six months or more is typically needed to move from intermediate to advanced. Many adults stop improving in specific areas despite years of exposure (fossilization, Selinker 1972), and fossilization is selective by feature. Millions of long-term US residents remain limited-English-proficient, so residence alone does not guarantee fluency.

### Cited Findings
- **Boren Awards report (Institute of International Education, 2015), 2,466 US students abroad.** Most studied abroad for **6+ months** and made an **average gain of two sublevels on the ACTFL scale**. For students starting at **Intermediate**, studying abroad **six months or longer is critical to reaching Advanced** — [IIE](https://www.iie.org/publications/the-boren-awards-a-report-of-oral-language-proficiency-gains/)
- **Initial proficiency.** Learners with higher initial L2 proficiency made greater L2 grammar gains from short-term immersion (Spanish summer programs) — [SSLA, Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/initial-proficiency-and-l2-grammar-development-during-shortterm-immersion-abroad/A573A756CB9F80398C1C5F4E67786E59)
- **Social networks.** A multi-program study of 100+ learners found that the strongest predictors of oral (ACTFL) gains were **cultural sensitivity and social-network variables**. Length of stay and language use are also cited in the literature — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/5893); [ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ891446.pdf)
- **Pre-departure grammar.** Students with greater grammatical skill before going abroad tend to make larger oral gains (earlier research cited in the same literature) — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/7916)
- **Immersion ceiling.** Immersion learners reach higher final grammar attainment than non-immersion learners, but attainment takes about 30 years — [Hartshorne et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6559801)
- **Fossilization (Selinker 1972).** Adults may fail to reach target-like competence "despite exposure, motivation, and practice". Errors persist, except in highly monitored tasks. **Han (2004; Selective Fossilization Hypothesis 2009)** argues fossilization is "local rather than global": a learner can master some features while others stay stuck — [SSLA, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/fossilization-in-adult-second-language-acquisition/F686B9A070A97F835F68D0BA96F8BC86); [Columbia TESOL working paper](https://journals.library.columbia.edu/index.php/SALT/article/download/1563/614/3895)
- **US data (Migration Policy Institute).**
  - Nearly **28 million US residents** (age 5+) reported limited English proficiency in 2023, and more than half were US citizens. MPI counts as LEP anyone who reports speaking English "not at all", "not well" or only "well" (rather than "very well") — [MPI data tool](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/data-tool/limited-english-proficient-persons); [MPI press release](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/press-release/mpi-releases-most-recent-national-and-state-data-immigrants-education-levels-and)
  - Among people speaking a non-English language at home, 40% were LEP in 2015, versus 44% in 1980 — [MPI](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/article/language-diversity-and-english-proficiency-united-states)

### Inferences
- For the Turkish adult moving to the US, the biggest controllable predictor is the size of their **English-speaking social network** and daily contact: work in English, American friends, community groups, volunteering. Living in a Turkish-speaking bubble (Turkish media, friends, workplace) reproduces non-immersion conditions.
- Arriving at a higher level (solid B1–B2) lets the person profit far more from immersion. Intensive preparation *before* the move is therefore high-value.
- Fossilized errors (articles, he/she, present perfect, the schwa) typically need deliberate work: explicit feedback, targeted SRS cards, shadowing and recordings. They don't fix themselves with exposure.

### Gaps
- No quantitative meta-analysis of study-abroad predictors was found. A specific MPI 2023 figure for immigrant LEP share, and a Turkish-born LEP share, were not retrieved.
- No studies were found specifically on Turkish immigrants' English attainment in the US.

## 9. Turkish speakers' specific difficulties with English (L1 transfer)

### Takeaway
Turkish EFL learner error studies consistently rank **articles**, **word choice/lexis**, **prepositions**, **word order** and **subject–verb agreement** as top error categories. **Tense/aspect, especially the present perfect**, is a known trouble spot because Turkish and English tenses don't map one-to-one. Pronunciation problems include **vowel epenthesis in consonant clusters** (e.g., /s/+stop onsets), **schwa**, **th** and **ng**, and the **/o/ vs. /oʊ/** contrast. How much of the article problem is L1 transfer is debated. The **he/she problem** (Turkish "o" is gender-neutral) is widely noted but I found **no empirical learner study** on it.

### Cited Findings
- **Error frequency (writing).** Kırmızı & Karcı (2017), essays by 30 second-year Karabük University students. **Articles were the most frequent error**, then word choice, prepositions, word order and subject–verb agreement. Causes: limited vocabulary, weak grammar knowledge and **L1 interference (the most common cause)**. Small sample **[WEAK–MODERATE]** — [Educational Process: International Journal (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/d6c3cb9e5247484b810aef5d80f6c14e)
- **Lexis.** Erkaya (2012, MEXTESOL Journal), analysis of 17 essays: lexical errors were "by far the most problematic" — [MEXTESOL](https://mextesol.net/journal/public/files/2aa772ff6244d053d5e096f1ff48aa14.pdf)
- **Articles (contrasting evidence).** Yılmaz (2006), Boğaziçi MA thesis. **No clear evidence of persistent L1 influence** on article use. Learners **overused "the" and "a/an" in zero-article contexts**, and variability decreased at advanced level. This conflicts with the "L1 interference" interpretation — [Boğaziçi Digital Archive](https://digitalarchive.library.bogazici.edu.tr/items/ac88fbc1-0d31-4ca9-b4a0-13ad5dd0b8f2/full)
- **Spoken errors.** Yıldız (2016, *International Journal of English Studies*) examined whether L1 Turkish causes interference errors in spoken English and whether proficiency affects error numbers — [IJES](https://revistas.um.es/ijes/article/download/212631/195731)
- **Present perfect and tense/aspect:**
  - Mergen (1999, Bilkent MA): "Turkish and English do not have one-to-one correspondence in terms of the present perfect and the present perfect continuous", and students tend to **translate directly from Turkish** — [Bilkent repository](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/a152df97-d9b1-42c3-9064-c1dcfb82592b)
  - A related analysis of Turkish students' writing: **present perfect used instead of simple past accounted for about 23% of verb tense/aspect errors** — [Bilkent repository (Aycan 1990 and related)](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/894b7ac6-d188-46c3-a957-be3b0ddda5e3)
  - Sezer (METU): 120 students at three proficiency levels; error types, sources and systematicity — [Bilkent repository](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/items/8fd8bf9c-fd73-432e-bd64-40600a3fc268)
  - Sarıoğlu (2023): 34 Turkish EFL learners. Present perfect use is constrained by verbs' **lexical aspect** and sentence context — [BTU repository](https://acikerisim.btu.edu.tr/items/3f628036-9326-49d8-9e7a-dddc9cddaf0f)
- **Prepositions (vs. Turkish case suffixes and postpositions):**
  - A study of 30 upper-intermediate prep-school students: "even at this level, students have great difficulty in finding the correct preposition", with many errors from mother-tongue interference — [ELT Research Journal (DergiPark)](https://dergipark.org.tr/tr/pub/eltrj/article/74451)
  - Erroneous **in / on / at** use "highly related to the [Turkish locative] postposition -de" (-de/-da), plus overgeneralization — [Erciyes Univ. corpus study](https://avesis.erciyes.edu.tr/yayin/9735f96c-3ebe-44b6-a8d3-ca0273b4eacf/a-corpus-based-study-on-the-preposition-error-types-in-turkish-efl-learners-essays)
  - A learner-corpus study analyses prepositional-verb errors across CEFR A1–C2 — [Çukurova Univ.](https://avesis.cu.edu.tr/yayin/f5511137-c426-4ab5-b6cc-a4360a0b1a63/contemporary-means-and-methods-in-elt-and-applied-linguistics)
- **Relative clauses.** Hacettepe (2020), 30 philology students. Recognition of English relative clauses varied significantly by relative-clause type, and explicit instruction greatly helped — [TR Dizin](https://search.trdizin.gov.tr/yayin/detay/395241)
  - An Anadolu University thesis calls relative clauses one of the most problematic areas for Turkish learners — [Anadolu Univ.](https://acikerisim.anadolu.edu.tr/bitstreams/0672fbc8-41c1-440d-b62a-630839c8019d/download)
- **Phrasal verbs (avoidance).** A study tested avoidance of English phrasal verbs among 400 Turkish ELT teacher trainees and 15 native speakers using three elicitation tests — [İKÜ open access](https://openaccess.iku.edu.tr/handle/11413/3613); [Tezara](https://tezara.org/theses/210098)
- **Collocations.** A 2023 study (43 participants, 24-item translation test) found L1 Turkish effects on English **verb collocations** — [Bayburt Univ.](https://openaccess.bayburt.edu.tr/items/d2a09de1-66ec-4f5a-a1d7-d6e2cc5293b8)
- **Pronoun "o".** Turkish has a single third-person pronoun, **"o"**, covering he, she and it; gender is inferred from context — [Elon.io Turkish grammar](https://elon.io/grammar/turkish/pronouns/personal); [Ling App](https://ling-app.com/blog/turkish-pronouns/)
  - Machine translation from Turkish must "guess" gender for "o", which illustrates the mapping problem — [Quartz](https://qz.com/1141122/google-translates-gender-bias-)
- **Pronunciation:**
  - **Clusters.** Word-initial consonant clusters "are not attested in the native lexicon of Turkish". Speakers insert vowels even in loanwords, e.g., colloquial epenthesis — [Canalis, ICPhS 2023](https://guarant.cz/icphs2023/745.pdf)
  - Yavaş & Altan (2016), 25 Turkish speakers. Epenthesis in English /s/-clusters was least for /st/ and /sn/; **/sp/ and /sk/ were the least successful** — [John Benjamins](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1075/slcs.175.01yav/html)
  - Geylanioğlu, 24 adult EFL learners: "serious difficulties in pronouncing **schwa, th and ng**" — [Academia](https://independent.academia.edu/SavasGeylanioglu)
  - Işık (2010): the **schwa is a fossilized pronunciation error** for Turks, attributed to mother-tongue interference, vowel reduction, connected speech and lack of professional instruction — [Hacettepe open access](https://openaccess.hacettepe.edu.tr/handle/11655/20578)
  - The **/o/ vs. /oʊ/ contrast** as a fossilized pronunciation error of Turkish learners — [Uşak Univ. AVESİS](https://avesis.usak.edu.tr/yayin/3b8d0191-f305-4227-a70d-c65e4b46cfe7/the-o-vs-ou-contrast-as-a-fossilized-pronunciation-error-of-turkish-learners-of-english-and-solutions-to-the-problem)

### Inferences
- Priority list of Turkish-specific targets, ranked by evidence of frequency:
  1. Articles (a/an/the/zero)
  2. Prepositions (in/on/at vs. -de/-da; dependent prepositions such as "depend **on**", "interested **in**")
  3. Present perfect vs. simple past
  4. Word choice, collocations and phrasal verbs
  5. Word order
  6. Subject–verb agreement (3rd-person -s)
  7. he/she
  8. Pronunciation: schwa, th, word-initial clusters (avoid "sı-tudent" for "student"), and stress and reduced vowels
- **Word order.** Turkish is basic SOV and English is SVO; the word-order errors reported by Kırmızı & Karcı are consistent with this. Relative clauses also differ (Turkish prenominal participle forms such as "-en/-dik" vs. English postnominal "who/which/that" clauses), consistent with the reported relative-clause difficulty. These typological facts were not separately sourced in this session.
- **Spelling and pronunciation.** Turkish spelling is highly phonemic, which plausibly drives spelling-pronunciation errors in English (pronouncing silent letters, mispronouncing reduced vowels). The schwa research supports this indirectly. Same-language captions (Section 4) and pronunciation-focused listening are logical countermeasures.
- **Grammatical gender.** The gender-neutral "o" predicts he/she slips in fluent speech even among advanced learners. This is typical "selective fossilization" (Han), so it is a target for deliberate monitoring and feedback.

### Gaps
- **No empirical study was found that quantifies he/she pronoun errors by Turkish learners of English**; the claim is based on L1 typology.
- No sourced list of Turkish–English **false friends** was retrieved; commonly cited examples such as "sempatik"/"sympathetic" and "aktüel"/"actual" are unverified here.
- No study was retrieved on /w/ vs. /v/ confusion among Turkish speakers.
- Most Turkish error-analysis studies are small (N = 17–120), single-institution and mostly about writing. Large learner-corpus quantifications (e.g., ICLE/TICLE) were not retrieved.

## 10. Turkey's English proficiency context: EF EPI and why Turkish learners plateau

### Takeaway
In the **EF EPI 2025** (published November 2025; 123 countries/territories), **Türkiye ranks 71st with a score of 488, down 9 points** from the previous edition. The global average is also 488, and Türkiye is in the "Low" proficiency band. **Speaking (438) and writing (441) are far weaker than reading (503)**, which fits the well-documented grammar- and exam-centered, low-communication school model. Students reportedly remain at a basic level after about 1,000 classroom hours.

### Cited Findings
- **EF EPI 2025 Türkiye fact sheet:**
  - Global ranking **#71**, EF EPI score **488 (−9)**, global average score **488**
  - Skills: **Reading 503, Listening 478, Writing 441, Speaking 438**
  - Scores by job function: Customer Service 538, Teacher 537, R&D 519, IT 513, Operations 487, Sales 482, Legal 477, Marketing 470, Accounting & Finance 445, Human Resources 441, Technicians & Maintenance 434, Students 489

- The EF EPI 2025 was launched in November 2025 and covers 123 countries and territories — [EF press release](https://www.ef.edu/about-us/press/articles/2025/ef-english-proficiency-index-2025-launched/)
- **Previous edition.** EF's regional page shows Türkiye **#65 of 116 with 497**, consistent with the 2024 edition and the −9 change. ### Inferences
- The Turkish adult profile is typically "knows grammar rules and can read, but cannot speak or understand fast speech". EF skill scores show speaking 438 vs. reading 503. The plateau is therefore mostly an **input-volume, output and interaction deficit**, not a grammar-knowledge deficit. This points to:
  - massive listening with English captions
  - daily speaking with feedback (tutors, language exchange, shadowing)
  - SRS for vocabulary and chunks
  - targeted work on the L1-transfer errors in Section 9
- The grammar-heavy background can be an *asset*. Explicit knowledge supports noticing and self-correction (Sections 1 and 5), but it must be proceduralized through practice (DeKeyser).
- Exam culture (YDS/YÖKDİL multiple-choice reading and grammar) rewards recognition, not production. This probably contributes to the reading–speaking gap; that is an inference, not tested here.
